TimeManager

The story of a personal time-tracking app, built end to end by Claude with Paul — from a locked design spec to a working Android app. Story up top, full technical record below.

The Story

09 August 2026

Day 1 — Laying the foundation

Milestone 1 · Scaffold

The design phase wrapped up today: every question about how the app should behave — break-law edge cases, how a running balance recalculates, what happens at midnight, what every screen looks like — got worked out in the Study vault before a single line of app code was written. That's the whole point of doing it in that order: nothing in this build should be an open design question anymore, just translation.

Today's job was pure groundwork: a real Flutter project now exists at ~/Code/time-manager, with its dependencies chosen (Drift for local storage, Riverpod for state, fl_chart for charts) and a folder structure that keeps the tricky, correctness-critical logic — the break-deduction math, the balance recalculation cascade — in plain Dart files with no Flutter dependency, so they can be tested in isolation before any UI touches them.

  • Folder structure laid out for data / domain / UI, kept separate on purpose
  • A placeholder screen confirms the app boots — nothing to look at yet

09 August 2026

The database comes online

Milestone 2 · Data layer

Every entity from the Data Model doc — sessions, breaks, leave, holidays, the daily summary, the running balance, vacation quotas, settings, and an audit trail — is now a real table in a real SQLite database on disk, nine of them in total.

One design decision worth calling out: the doc's "Design Principles" section says a day's summary record shouldn't exist until something actually happens on that day (a check-in, a leave request). Rather than trust the app to always remember that rule, it's now enforced by the database itself — a session or a leave entry literally cannot be saved for a day that doesn't have a summary record yet. Get the order wrong, and the save fails loudly instead of quietly corrupting data later.

Hit a snag: that rule wasn't actually being enforced A test written specifically to check the DayEntry-before-WorkSession rule passed... for the wrong reason — turns out SQLite ships with that kind of protection switched off unless you explicitly ask for it. One line (turning the protection on when the database opens) fixed it, and the test then failed correctly until the real rule was satisfied.
  • All 9 tables created, matching the design doc field-for-field
  • Data-integrity rule enforced at the database level, not just trusted to application code
  • 6 automated tests confirm the whole thing round-trips correctly

Next: the tricky part — the break-law math and balance recalculation logic.

09 August 2026

Teaching the app German labor law

Milestone 3 · Domain engines

This was the milestone the whole design phase existed to protect: the math that decides how much of a work day actually counts, automatically, the way German law requires. Five self-contained pieces of logic went in today, each with no dependency on the app's UI at all — they're just functions that take numbers in and give numbers out, which means they can be tested exhaustively without ever launching the app.

  • Break law: works out how much unpaid break a day legally needs (30 minutes past 6 hours worked, 45 past 9), checks whether the real gaps between sessions already cover it, and if not, decides exactly where on the timeline to silently add the difference.
  • Daily targets & balance: turns a weekly-hours goal into a daily one, adjusts it for holidays, and rolls each day's over/under into the running balance.
  • Midnight cutoff: if you forget to check out and it rolls past midnight, the app knows to stop the clock.
  • German holidays: calculated from scratch using a 200-year-old mathematical method for pinning down Easter each year (everything else — Christmas, German Unity Day — cascades off that one date), rather than depending on a third-party list that could go stale.
  • Vacation rollover: carries unused vacation days into the next year under whichever policy is configured, and warns before they'd expire.

The break-law calculation was checked directly against the worked example from the design notes — an 8am-to-2:30pm day with a 20-minute lunch — and it landed on exactly the same numbers by hand: a 30-minute break requirement, a 10-minute shortfall, and 6 hours flat of counted work. The German-holiday math was double-checked against 1954 and 1981, two years famous (among people who calculate Easter dates, anyway) for breaking naive versions of this algorithm — both came out correct.

One judgment call worth flagging The design notes describe extending a too-short break "to cover the required minimum," which is exactly what happens when there's only one break in the day. But if a day had several separate breaks, it's genuinely ambiguous whether that phrase means "top up the shortfall" or "make this specific gap exactly the legal minimum." Went with "top up the shortfall," since it gives the right answer either way and is safer if a day ever has multiple breaks — flagged in the code for a second look later if it ever matters in practice.

33 new tests, all passing. Next: wiring all of this up to the database so the app can actually react live as data changes.

09 August 2026

Wiring the brain to the body

Milestone 4 · Riverpod wiring

The last three milestones each stood alone: a database that stores things, and a set of calculators that don't know the database exists. Today they got connected — every check-in, check-out, vacation day, and settings change now flows through one place that recomputes exactly what needs recomputing and nothing more, then the whole thing was hooked up to Riverpod so the (still-unbuilt) UI will just be able to watch the numbers update live.

This is also where the app picked up its two most important safety nets: only one active session at a time, and every mutation writes an audit trail entry — the two rules the whole "personal record you can trust" idea depends on.

A bug the tests caught before it ever ran Early on, the recalculation logic would have quietly created a database entry for every single day it touched — including days with absolutely nothing on them. That directly breaks a rule from the design phase: a day's record should only exist once something actually happens on it. Caught this by re-reading the design notes against the code before writing any tests, not by a test failing — worth noting since it's the kind of bug that's invisible until someone notices years of empty placeholder days cluttering their history.
Why 4 of 6 new tests failed on the first run — and what it revealed Every failure looked different on the surface (different numbers, different fields) but traced back to the same cause: the recalculation logic was reading the real current date directly off the system clock. That's normally invisible, but it meant the exact moment the tests happened to run could quietly change their outcome — a classic "works on my machine, breaks in six months" bug. The fix was to make "what day is it" something the tests can control explicitly instead of leaving it to chance, which is a good habit for any code that has a notion of "today."

45 tests passing across the whole app now. Next: before writing a single screen, a full design-system and page-by-page spec — the one deliberate pause in this build, so the visual direction gets nailed down properly instead of guessed at.

Pausing on purpose

Milestone 5 · Design system & screen spec

Everything so far has been invisible — a database, some math, some wiring. This milestone is different: it's the one deliberate stop in the whole build. Before a single screen gets built, the plan calls for writing up exactly what every screen needs to do and show, and handing that over externally to get the actual look — colors, type, spacing, motion — nailed down properly, since "looks and feels modern and visually aesthetic" is one of the app's stated goals and that's not something to guess at while also writing business logic.

The document that came out of this is written as a brief, not a finished design system: every screen's structure and behavior is already fixed by the earlier design discussion (what's on the Home screen, how History drills from month to day, what the Stats charts are), but the actual visual language — the parts a design tool is good at — is deliberately left as open questions rather than guessed here.

09 August 2026

The app gets a face

Milestone 6 · Core UI

The design handoff came back from Claude Design — a "Nocturne" visual language with a soft lavender-tinted background, outlined (never filled) buttons, and a muted sage green for breaks against a warm amber for warnings. Every color in it was specified in OKLCH, a color space Flutter doesn't understand natively, so the first job was porting the math (Björn Ottosson's OKLab matrices) into plain Dart before a single screen could be built — otherwise the greens and ambers would come out looking nothing like the mockups.

With the color system, type scale, and shared building blocks (the progress ring, the timeline chips, the bottom nav) in place, the six screens came together in roughly the order someone would actually meet them: onboarding wizard, Home, History, Settings, then the Stats shell (its charts are Milestone 8 — today it's the tab bar, range chips, and "coming soon" cards, built now so the navigation is whole).

Discovery: "real" breaks were never supposed to be stored Building Home's timeline surfaced something the earlier milestones had gotten right without a UI to prove it: a break the user never touched (just the natural gap between clocking out for lunch and back in) isn't a database row anywhere — only the auto-inserted legal breaks are. The timeline has to notice the gaps itself. That became a small new pure-Dart module (timeline_builder.dart, unit tested) that merges sessions and synthetic breaks and fills in whatever's left over as a plain break — no schema changes needed, the data was already sufficient.
Problem: onboarding's starting balance had nowhere to live The Settings table was never designed to hold a one-time "carry over this many hours" number — and it shouldn't be, since it's a fact about a date, not an ongoing preference. Fix: seed it as a pre-dated balance snapshot instead, the same mechanism the recalculation cascade already uses to find "the last known balance before today." Zero changes to the cascade logic itself.

Ran the whole thing on the emulator afterward: fresh install → 4-step onboarding → check in → watched the ring tick live → checked out → balance updated → drilled History from Month down to a single week → Settings reflected exactly what onboarding had recorded → the Stats shell's tabs and range chips all switch cleanly. One good scare along the way — the "Continue" button looked unresponsive for a good ten minutes of tapping, until it turned out the coordinates being tapped were nowhere near the actual button (measured in the wrong screen-coordinate space); the button itself was fine the whole time.

50 tests passing, flutter analyze clean, golden path verified end-to-end on the emulator. Next: the home-screen widget — a native Kotlin + Glance surface reading the same database.

Stepping outside Flutter

Milestone 7 · Android widget

Everything so far has been one Flutter app. This milestone is a second, much smaller program living inside the same APK: a native Android home-screen widget, written in Kotlin against Jetpack Glance, that reads the same SQLite file the Flutter app writes to. No shared code, no shared framework — just an agreement about where the data lives and what it means.

Before writing a line of it, the actual Glance library that ships in this version got pulled apart rather than trusted from memory — Gradle was told to fetch it, and the real compiled classes got inspected directly. Good thing too: Glance's built-in progress ring turned out to be decorative only, with no way to show a partial fill. The fix was to draw the ring by hand — the same round-capped-arc math as the Flutter version, just aimed at an Android Canvas instead of Flutter's — and hand Glance a finished picture instead of asking it to draw one.

The tap did nothing, and both sides of the bug looked innocent For a while, tapping the widget produced no error, no crash, and no visible change — just silence. The trap: the widget's read path was also subtly broken, computing the database's location one folder too deep, but it failed in a way that looked correct — an empty, not-yet-onboarded app also shows a blank "0:00," so the wrong-path bug and the right answer were indistinguishable at a glance. Tracing it required checking the real file location on the device rather than trusting the assumption carried over from the Flutter side, which turned out to be off by exactly one directory level. Fixed, and confirmed properly this time — pulling the actual database off the device after tapping, not just eyeballing the screen.

One more thing worth naming rather than glossing over: the design called for three ring colors — tracking, on a break, checked out — but the app's data model only ever records a session starting and stopping, never a break beginning. "On a break" had to be inferred (no active session, but some work already logged today, short of the target) rather than read directly, the same kind of judgment call made a few times already in this build.

Verified on the emulator: added to the home screen through the real widget picker (not simulated), tapped to check in, confirmed a genuine session row appeared in the database, tapped again to check out. flutter analyze clean, debug build succeeds with the widget included. Nine charts, none of them the same shape

Milestone 8 · Stats & charts

The Stats screen had been sitting there since Milestone 6 with six "Coming in Milestone 8" placeholders. This milestone replaced every one of them with a real chart wired to real data — three on Overview (balance trend, weekly target hit rate, overtime accumulation rate), four on Patterns (monthly heatmap, daily hours, hours by day of week, check-in-time distribution), and Leave's vacation/sick breakdown. Two of those — the heatmap and the check-in histogram — have no off-the-shelf shape in fl_chart, exactly as the design spec called out, so they're hand-drawn CustomPainters instead. The Leave donut turned out not to need a new chart at all: the ProgressRing widget built back in Milestone 6 for Home's daily-target ring was already the exact shape this screen needed, doc comment and all — a small case of earlier work paying for itself twice.

Most of the actual math lives in a new, small, pure-Dart module rather than inside the widgets: trailing date ranges ("last 30 days," not calendar-aligned), grouping days into ISO weeks, and the weekday/hour aggregations behind the bar charts and the histogram — kept separate and unit tested the same way the break-law engine was back in Milestone 3, so the chart widgets themselves stay dumb rendering code. One deliberate scope call: the monthly heatmap ignores the global Month/6 Months/Year range selector and always shows a single calendar month with its own prev/next arrows — a flattened six-month window doesn't lay out as a legible calendar grid, and Leave's tab already established the precedent of a chart with its own independent range control.

Every tab in the bottom nav stopped responding — except it hadn't After wiring everything up, tapping History, Stats, or Settings in the bottom nav did nothing at all — no error, no crash, just the Home tab staying put. The instinct was to suspect the new IndexedStack children or a swallowed exception in StatsScreen. Neither was it: the bug was in the testing, not the app. Screenshots get shown scaled down to 900×2000 for review, and the habit from earlier milestones was to eyeball tap coordinates off that preview and scale by 1.2×. That math is right for the content, but the bottom nav bar sits right at the bottom edge, and a rounding mismatch meant the computed tap landed about 560 pixels above where the nav bar actually is — square in the middle of the scrollable content instead. Measuring the real 1080×2400 screenshot directly (searching for the accent purple of the active tab icon) found the nav row's true position, and every tab, range chip, and the Leave year selector confirmed working immediately after.

The last milestone: tying off loose ends

Milestone 9 · Polish & release build

Nine milestones in, most of "polish" turned out to already be done. The audit log screen was built back in Milestone 6, ahead of its actual milestone, and just needed confirming it still works. Dark mode was wired since Milestone 6 too — every screen reads color off AppColors.light/AppColors.dark rather than a hardcoded value — but had never actually been looked at. Moving in for real: five months of history, and a landmine in the calendar math

Post-launch · Real data import & a real bug

With the build finished, the next step was making it Paul's actual system of record instead of a demo: importing every day worked since 15 March 2026 — two source PDFs (an internal project-hours sheet covering March–May, and a public time-tracker export with real check-in/out timestamps from 20 April onward, preferred wherever both had data), plus sick/vacation days and two blocks of already-planned future leave. Rather than hand-writing SQL, the import ran through the app's own repositories and RecalculationService — a standalone Dart script temporarily borrowing AppDatabase with a file-backed executor, so the imported history is produced by exactly the same code path a real check-in/check-out would use, break-law deduction included.

Every day after 27 March came back zeroed The first import run computed a wildly wrong total (78 hours instead of ~725) and a nonsense balance, with every DayEntry from 30 March onward stuck at net=0/target=0 despite real sessions being in the database. The cutoff was suspicious: exactly 10 correct days, then nothing — and 29 March 2026 is Germany's spring-forward DST transition. RecalculationService's day-stepping loops used date.add(Duration(days: 1)) to walk the calendar. Duration arithmetic operates on the underlying instant, not the wall-clock date — adding 24 real hours across a spring-forward boundary lands on 01:00 local, not midnight. Every date.equals(...) lookup downstream of that point then silently misses, forever (the offset never self-corrects until the next DST transition). This is a real bug in the shipped app, not just the import script — anyone using TimeManager across a DST change would have hit the same silent freeze. Fixed with a calendar-safe shiftDays() helper (domain/date_only.dart) that reconstructs the date via its year/month/day components instead of adding a Duration, applied everywhere a local date gets stepped by N days: both RecalculationService loops, stats_aggregation.dart's trailing-range and week-start math, midnight_cutoff.dart, History's week/month navigation, the Stats custom date-range picker, and holiday_calculator.dart's Easter-offset holidays (latent for 2026, but the same landmine for a year where Easter falls close to the boundary). Added a regression test asserting shiftDays stays exact across the exact date that broke it. Re-running the import after the fix landed on numbers that checked out exactly against the source PDFs' own totals.

Also wired up PublicHolidayRepository.seedYear, which existed since Milestone 4 but was never actually called from anywhere — meaning every German public holiday, forever, would have silently required a full day's target hours instead of zero. It now runs once per app start (idempotent) for the current and next year.

Getting the seeded database onto the phone hit one more snag: adb run-as (needed to write into the app's private storage without root) only works on a debuggable build, and flutter build apk --release forces debuggable=false no matter what the Gradle config says. Worked around it by temporarily signing the debug build type with the same release keystore — a debuggable build that still shares the installed app's signing certificate installs in place, no uninstall, no data loss — pushed the database, then rebuilt and reinstalled the real non-debuggable release over it, again in place. 62/62 tests passing (58 plus the new DST regression tests), flutter analyze clean, and the on-device balance, History, and Settings screens all verified against the independently-computed expected values.

Living with it: seven real bugs, a holiday problem, and a corrupted balance

Post-launch · Real-world usage bugs

With ten days of real daily use behind it, Paul logged seven problems he'd actually hit (Study/Projects/TimeManager/Problems.md): check-in not updating the ring/target/remaining-time until checkout, the Public Holidays and Vacation Quota settings rows being dead stubs, 4 June (Fronleichnam, a Baden-Württemberg-only holiday) showing as a missed workday, History having no visual distinction between holiday/vacation/sick/worked days, and no way to edit or delete a session once created. All seven root causes traced back to logic that already existed correctly somewhere in the app but was never wired up to the screen that needed it — most notably checkIn() never calling recalculateFrom() the way every other mutation does, and germanNationalHolidays() deliberately excluding regional holidays because the plan had assumed Paul would add them by hand. He didn't want that: holidays needed to just know he's in Baden-Württemberg. Added badenWuerttembergHolidays() (Heilige Drei Könige, Fronleichnam, Allerheiligen) and folded it into seedYear() alongside the nationwide list, plus built out the previously-dead Settings screens, holiday-aware History coloring, and a session/manual-break editing sheet.

Shipped it to Paul's phone (in place, keystore-verified, firstInstallTime confirmed unchanged) and he came back within minutes: Fronleichnam now correctly showed 0 delta, but Home's overall Balance was still frozen at the old wrong number — eight hours short of what fixing that one day should have restored. Nothing in the code read wrong on paper. Chasing it required temporarily instrumenting the buried Audit Log screen to dump raw BalanceSnapshot rows directly off the device (a release build can't be inspected any other way without root), which turned up the actual cause: _cascadeBalanceFrom had always written a snapshot for whatever date it was recalculating, including future dates like upcoming holidays — so the original nationwide-only holiday seed on 9 August had planted a stale snapshot out at December 2027, and watchLatest()'s ORDER BY date DESC kept returning that stray future row instead of today's real balance, no matter how correctly the past got recalculated. Fixed by making future dates a no-op for snapshot writes and purging the already-corrupted rows on startup. Balance corrected live, on-device, from -33:42 to -25:04 — exactly the eight hours the Fronleichnam fix should have restored.

The diagnostic panel was fully reverted afterward (confirmed via a clean git diff on that file). Lesson kept in memory for next time: a per-date fix reading correctly on paper doesn't prove it propagated — for anything touching the balance cascade, verify the actual number on the device, not just the day's own entry.

A widget that only knew one season

Post-launch · Widget sizing & dark mode

Two more real complaints from daily use: the home-screen widget only ever offered the 2x2 footprint at placement time (no way to drop a smaller 1x1 tile), and it always rendered the light palette regardless of the phone's system theme — a deliberate scope call from the Milestone 7 build ("one consistent look reads better than a day/night swap most launchers don't reliably signal to widgets anyway"), but not one Paul wanted kept. Fixed the sizing by widening time_manager_widget_info.xml's minWidth/targetCellWidth down to a true 1-cell footprint and adding a third SizeMode.Responsive tier below the existing compact/expanded pair, with its own (smaller) ring and type scale.

Dark mode turned out to need real investigation rather than a one-line fix: Jetpack Glance 1.1.1 has no public day/night ColorProvider (confirmed by decompiling the actual library classes rather than trusting memory — the only day/night color mechanism in that version is internal, used by its own checkbox/switch translators) and no determinate circular progress composable, so the ring stays a hand-rendered bitmap that can't defer its color resolution to the launcher process the way a resource-id-backed color could. Resolved the whole palette — background, text, track, and the ring itself — uniformly from the system's current uiMode once per render, so the widget never shows a half-flipped, inconsistent mix of old and new colors. The tradeoff, noted honestly rather than glossed over: a bare system theme toggle with nothing else happening can lag up to the existing 30-minute refresh window, since Android doesn't deliver ACTION_CONFIGURATION_CHANGED to a manifest-declared widget receiver at all — every check-in/out tap already forces an immediate refresh via updateAll, which covers the common case.

Verified end to end: keystore signatures matched before and after the rebuild, firstInstallTime unchanged after adb install -r, and Glance's own SessionWorker completing successfully in logcat with no crash on the first post-install render. The widget's actual on-screen appearance in both new sizes and both themes still needs Paul's own eyes — it wasn't visible on the first few home-screen pages checked, and repositioning a widget on someone's real home screen isn't something to automate.

10 August 2026

One ring to fill them all

Post-launch · Progress ring redesign

Paul's next piece of feedback, right after the widget sizing/dark mode fix: Home's progress ring and the separate linear "Daily target" bar underneath it were both showing the same number in two different shapes, and neither felt like it was actually filling — the bar just snapped to a new value whenever something recalculated, and the widget's ring only ever changed on a check-in/out tap. He wanted one indicator, not two: the ring should be the fill, it should visibly creep forward on its own rather than jump, and once the daily target is hit it should keep going around — overtime as another lap of the same ring, each lap one shade darker than the last, instead of stopping dead at 100%.

Dropped the linear bar entirely (kept the "Daily target 8:38 / 7:54"-style text above it, just not its own progress fill) and wrote the lap math once, in parallel, on both sides of the Dart/Kotlin boundary this project always has to straddle: lib/domain/lap_progress.dart and the widget's new LapProgress.kt both turn a raw worked/target ratio into "which lap, how far through it" — a ratio of exactly 1.0 reads as a full lap 0, not an empty lap 1, so the ring never flickers back to zero right at the target. Darkening per lap is HSL lightness scaling on the Dart side (core/theme/color_space.dart) and the platform's built-in HSV value scaling on the Kotlin side (no new dependency needed) — not pixel-identical between the two, but both read as "one shade deeper each lap," which is all the cue needs to do.

Home's ring was already ticking once a second while a session is active, so "fills continuously" was mostly free there. The widget was the real gap — a home-screen widget has no way to just animate on its own, and the OS won't let a manifest-declared widget refresh faster than every 30 minutes on its own periodic schedule. Worked around it with a small self-rescheduling WorkManager chain (WidgetRefreshWorker): a one-shot work request that redraws the widget, checks whether a session is still active, and if so enqueues another copy of itself a minute later — started on check-in, and it stops on its own at checkout rather than needing an explicit cancel. provideGlance also opportunistically resumes the chain any time it renders and finds a session active, so check-ins started from the app itself (which has no direct hook into the widget process) or a reboot mid-session both self-heal within, at worst, the existing 30-minute window. Honest caveat carried over from the sizing/dark-mode fix: WorkManager ticks are best-effort under Doze/battery optimization, not a hard guarantee of exactly once a minute — but they always catch up, never drift forever.

Verified the same way as the previous widget change: 79/79 tests passing (including new coverage for the lap math and the darkening curve), flutter analyze clean, keystore signature matched before and after the rebuild, firstInstallTime unchanged after adb install -r, no crash anywhere in logcat after launch. This time a real on-device screenshot of Home confirmed the bar is gone and the ring shows a small darkened sliver for the 44 minutes of that day's overtime — the widget's own on-screen look (both the lap-darkening and the once-a-minute tick) still needs Paul's own eyes, the same standing limitation as ever for a widget that isn't visible from this environment's home-screen checks.

10 August 2026

A rail with only one dot on it

Post-launch · Home timeline redesign

Right after the progress ring change, one more piece of feedback about the same screen: Home's "Today" list only ever put a dot at the very first check-in of the day — everything after that (a checkout, a break, checking back in, the final checkout) was just a run of identically-styled chips hanging off one long gray line, with no visual sense of it being a sequence of actual moments in the day. Paul wanted a dot at every check-in and check-out, and the connecting lines between them styled distinctly enough to read as a real timeline rather than a decorative sidebar.

The fix is mostly a reframing rather than new data: `buildDayTimeline` already produces the alternating work/break blocks that drive the chips, so every block boundary is a check-in or check-out — the only change needed was to stop only drawing a dot at the very first one. Each dot now carries its own clock time and a "Check in"/"Check out" label (derived from whether the block that starts there is work or a break), and the rail segment between two dots is colored and dashed to match the block spanning them — solid lavender for work, a looser teal dash for a real break, a fine muted dot-dash for a synthetic one — so the dot's own color previews what's coming next before you even read the chip label below it. A currently-active session still ends the rail with the familiar pulsing dot; if there's a gap between the last checkout and resuming (an in-progress break that hasn't been checked back in from yet), that now shows up as its own dashed segment too, rather than silently vanishing between two dots.

Building it exposed a genuine, easy-to-miss Flutter footgun along the way: the very first on-device screenshot showed the dots connected by only a stub of line a few pixels long, not a continuous line reaching the next dot — the vertical connector line was an `Expanded` sitting inside a `Column` that gets an effectively unbounded height (a plain `Row` placed directly in a `ListView` is handed infinite max-height by the scroll axis), so it had nothing finite to fill and collapsed. Wrapping each rail row in `IntrinsicHeight` — which forces a real, bounded height from the row's own content before doling constraints back out — fixed it outright; re-verified with a fresh on-device screenshot showing continuous, correctly colored lines all the way down.

22 September 2026

Golden Hour: the redesign starts with a backup, and hits a toolchain wall

Redesign · Phase 0

The app works, but it has always looked like stock Material 3 with a lavender seed colour. Claude Design turned the Milestone 5 spec into a full visual system — "Golden Hour": warm paper in light, a deep dusk in dark, an apricot tracking colour, a lilac break colour, and a deep-violet "Dusk Slab" on Home carrying the balance as the biggest number in the app with the ring cut into it. It came with a 879-line implementation handoff and 74 renders, which is what the next stretch of work implements.

Before any of that, the first job was the thing that has been missing since day one: a way to get the data off the phone. The release build is not debuggable, so adb run-as cannot read the database, and the only backup on disk was from 9 August — six weeks of real check-ins behind. 24 September 2026

Golden Hour: getting out of the toolchain, then rebuilding every screen

Redesign · Phases 1–3

The last entry ended with nothing able to build. Two dependencies had not survived the jump from Flutter 3.35 to 3.47, and neither had a newer version to move to. The way out was to stop depending on them: the Phosphor icon font is now bundled as an asset with the twenty-odd glyphs the design actually uses declared by hand, and Riverpod's code generation is gone entirely, its providers written out longhand. Both were removals rather than upgrades, which is why the app compiles on an SDK neither package supports.

Then a decision that shaped everything after it. A redesign of seven screens needs to be looked at, constantly, in two themes — and this machine cannot run an emulator and a Gradle build at the same time without the desktop falling over. So the app learned to draw itself into PNG files from a test: about six seconds for every designed state, no device, no database. That only works if a screen can be built from plain values rather than from live providers, which is why each one was split into a half that fetches and a half that draws. It sounds like bookkeeping. It is the reason the next six phases were verifiable at all.

With that in place the shell was rebuilt around a floating pill nav, and the pieces the screens are made of — the Sun Dial, the timeline, the day row in its nine states, the dashed and hatched borders — became a component sheet that renders on its own. The first real bug of the redesign showed up here and was pure Flutter: a row stretching its children inside a list, which has no bounded height to stretch into, overflowed by ninety-nine thousand pixels. The fix retired an IntrinsicHeight construction that an earlier entry in this log had been rather pleased with.

What a real database did to a design that had never met one

History now counts it too. And the designed "checked out" state turned out to be almost unreachable: a gap of under two hours had been reading as a break, so leaving at 18:51 still claimed you were on a break at 20:00. The third finding was about volume rather than correctness. The balance had been past its floor for three weeks, so every single History row wore the warning treatment and explained itself — fifteen rows all replacing a useful time span with the same sentence. Subtraction, two clocks that should not have been there, and shipping

28 September 2026

The first week of actually using it

First-use pass

The redesign shipped to the phone in the morning and was used as a real tracker for the rest of the day. By the evening there were eight complaints, and the most interesting one was not a rendering bug at all: Home said today was a vacation day, and it was right. Seventeen leave entries had come in with the August import, three of them for 28, 29 and 30 September — a plan made six weeks earlier and quietly abandoned. The app had been crediting the balance for both the vacation and the work done on top of it, which is where the headline number's sudden five-hour improvement had come from.

And there was nowhere to fix it. The repository had a working addLeave and deleteLeave, audited and recalculating, with no caller anywhere in the app — leave could arrive from an import and then never change, while Settings went on offering a vacation quota for it to count against. That is now a long-press on any day in History and a year list under Settings, and an entry is always a fraction of that date's own target rather than a typed number of hours.

The striped bar under the balance was the same story seen from the other end. It was a composition bar on a wall-clock axis with leave appended to it, and the design document said in so many words that it was not a progress bar — but it sits directly under "5:49 of 7:54 · 2:05 left", full width, rounded like every progress bar ever drawn. The phantom vacation day took fifty-four per cent of it, so a day that was seventy-three per cent worked read as about a third. It stacks against the target now, and the empty part of it is the number written above it.

Two findings were self-inflicted. Every work-session chip on Home had drifted into the middle of the screen while breaks and leave stayed flush left, because a wrapper added four days earlier to grow tap targets centred its child — and the render fixtures passed no tap callbacks, so no reference image had ever shown a tappable chip. And the History year stepper threw away the month every time it moved, so changing the year and then switching to Week landed you in January. Both had been in front of the camera the whole time and neither was in frame.

28 September 2026

The day that would not end

Second-use pass

Four more complaints arrived the same evening, noted before the morning's build had shipped, with the instruction to check whether they still applied. One did not — the day bar had already been taught to rescale past the target — which is worth more than it sounds: it is the first time a report has been answered by the previous fix rather than by a new one.

The other three all wanted the same missing idea. The balance dropped by a whole day the moment you checked in, because an untracked workday is worth minus its target and today counted as untracked until the hours were in. A morning of work read as a catastrophe and a finished day read as normal, which is exactly backwards: the morning is the part that is still going to be fixed. The app had no way to say "this day is not over yet". It had no way to say "this day is over", either — nothing settled yesterday, nothing stopped a session left running overnight, and a function written months ago to do precisely that had never once been called.

The rule that came out of it was the owner's, not the programmer's: outside normal working hours, an idle hour means you have gone home, and midnight means it regardless. What made it cheap was the sentence he added after it — that a day judged wrongly costs nothing, because it only affects the number on the screen. That settled the architecture on the spot. Nothing is persisted, no column was added to mark a day closed, and the recorded balance of every past day is exactly what it was. The only thing that changed is what gets printed, out of one pair of pure functions that four screens and one launcher widget all read.

The timer had the same shape of problem. It switched between three unrelated stopwatches depending on what was running, and because a break is stored as a check-out followed by a check-in, both anchors moved to "now" at each boundary — so the big number reset to zero twice per break. Taking lunch made the whole morning disappear from the one figure that gets looked at most. It is the day's total now, in every state, and the break clock moved down to the caption where it shares the line with nothing.

The last one was the plainest of the four. Vacation is almost never one day, and booking a fortnight meant fourteen trips through a date picker and a sheet. It is a range now: tap the first day, tap the last, tap anything between to drop it. Weekends cannot be tapped at all, and the footer counts what is actually being booked — which is the number that tells you whether "the twelfth to the twenty-third" is the ten days you meant.
